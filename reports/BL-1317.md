## The answer, in plain language

You asked how many accounts have to be fetched to get 1,000 pages you want, how many get
judged, how many emails come out, and what it costs. Here it is.

**On Instagram, working through pages we already have, you fetch about 3,700 accounts, judge
all of them, keep 1,000, and get about 169 addresses — of which about 152 reach a real person
rather than a shared desk. It costs $4.48 and about 15 hours of judging.**

**On brand-new Instagram accounts nobody has looked at, the same 1,000 pages cost you 11,400
fetches, $13.92, and 46 hours** — because only 9 pages in 100 are ones you want, against 28 in
100 in the stock we already hold. That is the same money per account; the difference is
entirely the hit rate.

**On TikTok you fetch about 4,700 and judge only 2,700**, because a free freshness check throws
away 43% before anything is bought. It is the cheapest per wanted page at $2.39.

**And then the thing you should know before spending another hour on TikTok emails:**

> ### Not one of the 37 TikTok pages you marked WANT publishes an email address.
> 0 of 37, and your REJECTs run 9 of 63. **The pages that publish an address are the promo and
> brand pages your own rules throw away.** So "1,000 wanted TikTok pages" currently converts to
> **zero emails**, not the 106 the headline rate implies.

## What the numbers mean, in your terms

**Money is not your problem.** A full week of Instagram at eight hours a day costs about
**$12**. You could double every vendor price and it would not change a decision.

**Your two real costs are hours and plan capacity.** Judging is the slow part on Instagram, and
one thousand wanted pages from fresh discovery is **46 hours of it** — more than a working
week. From stock it is **15 hours**, which is a comfortable two days.

**Working stock is three times cheaper than buying new supply, on every axis** — because 28 in
100 are wanted against 9 in 100. Same cost per account, three times the hit rate. When the
stock runs out, everything gets three times worse at once.

**TikTok is the cheapest per wanted page and the worst per email** — by a wide margin, and on
your own marks it does not produce emails at all.

## §2 — The $4.38 / $13.33 question: there was never a conflict

My brief quoted **$4.38**; BL-1312 published **$13.33**. They are the same model at two
different hit rates:

```
cost ratio  13.61 / 4.38 = 3.1111…
rate ratio   0.280 / 0.090 = 3.1111…      identical to ten decimals
```

That identity is necessary, not lucky: under the shipped order the cost is exactly
`unit ÷ want rate`, so the cost ratio **is** the inverse rate ratio. There is no room for a
second model. **$4.38 prices working stock; $13.33 prices buying new supply.** The 2.1%
residual between $13.33 and $13.61 is BL-1312 rounding the unit cost to two significant
figures — its own table already prints $4.29 one line above the $4.38 it labels "the figure
planning uses today".

**My table says $4.48 and $13.92, slightly above both**, because it adds a real measured loss
the older figures omitted: pooled over four capture logs, **308 of 315 fetches produced a grid**
(97.8%). Seven pages were fetched, billed, and yielded nothing to judge. That is a genuine
2.2% instrument loss.

> **And a correction to BL-1309 in the other direction.** It derived *"15.0 pages fetched per
> wanted page"* from BL-1296's 135-captured / 100-kept. But 135 was a **pre-registered sampling
> margin** — capture 135, keep the first 100 in hash order — not a 35% loss. Reading it as a
> loss inflates every Instagram dollar by 35%. The real figure is 11.4.

**Every Instagram rate is on a filter-passed population; every TikTok rate is on all profiled
pages.** They are not interchangeable, and they have never been printed side by side with
their denominators until now.

### And on TikTok, where both quantities exist on one population, the join runs backwards

| | with an address | n | P(email \| verdict) |
|---|---:|---:|---:|
| **You say WANT** | **0** | **37** | **0.0%** [0.0, 9.4] |
| You say REJECT | 9 | 63 | 14.3% [7.7, 25.0] |

Fisher exact **p = 0.024**. Independently reproduced against the judge's verdicts: WANT 2/77 =
2.6%, not-WANT 15/82 = 18.3%, **p = 0.0015**.

**Publishing an address correlates with being the promo or brand page the rubric kills.**
Sizing TikTok off the 10.6% discovery rate gives 25.5 accounts per address — **wrong by 4.1×,
and wrong in the flattering direction.**

### The ceiling on Instagram is structural, not a parser bug

Of 300 addressless Instagram pages, **230 (76.7%) publish no followable link at all.** There is
nothing to scrape. Today's rate is 16.9%; adding every measured path takes it to **17.2%**; the
counterfactual ceiling if *every* followable link yielded an address is **36.3%**. The
remaining **63.7% is structurally unreachable.**

**The business field is 51% of the yield** — 31 of 61 addresses come from the contact button
alone. It was stamped with the same `instagram:bio` label as bio text, so **every pre-BL-1179
statement that "it all comes from the bio" was describing a label, not the data.** Two rounds
concluded an untried path existed when it was already wired and already the bigger half.

### The poisoned pair, confirmed poisoned

**59.1% bioLink** and **27.8% cross-link** come from a 115-row **send file** — and that file's
own any-email rate is **113 of 115 = 98.3%**, because every row was selected for already having
an address. Fresh equivalents: bioLink **17.0%**, cross-link **1.9%**. Inflation **3.5×** and
**15×**. Neither is a base rate and neither should be quoted as one.

### Of the addresses that do come out, 1 in 10 is not a person

**725 of 7,345 addressed rows (9.9%) are shared desks** — support@, info@ — rising to **19.7%
on the meme funnel.** So "1,000 emails" is about **900 people**, and about 800 on meme pages.

### Corrections to the throughput figures, because three of them are stale

- **"Judging is the clock" is an Instagram statement.** On TikTok the **vendor fetch is slower
  than judging** — 5.05 profiles/min against 6.46 — so TikTok binds on the vendor, not on you.
- **The 6.46 pages/min was measured at 1 page per agent** — the configuration that batching
  replaced. **No round has ever timed the config that actually ships** (free sub-agents, 8
  pages each). That is the single missing measurement in this whole model.
- **Capture's 123.22 pages/min was never shipped.** Nothing imports the fast path; the shipped
  script still carries 7.2 seconds of sleeps, and production runs 2 lanes at **10.59**. So
  capture leads judging by **1.6×, not 12×**.
- **8,548 tokens/page is a lab best case** (n=3 agents). Two production runs measure **11,786**
  and **12,393**. The "117 pages per million" figure **overstates capacity by 38%.**

### And the 768-a-day ceiling is not a measured send

It is arithmetic: `14,659 judged/24 h × 49.5% × 10.6%`. **No email was transmitted and no rate
limit was probed.** It assumes 24 hours continuous, uses the paid judging rate on a path that
cannot run unattended, uses the **dead 49.5%**, and applies a **profiled-page** email rate to
post-WANT pages. **And today the real send rate is zero** — BL-1314 found `send_identity.json`
does not exist, so every message refuses.

**What actually binds first:** dollars never ($12/week). Hours bind visibly. **Tokens bind
first on Instagram** — an 8-hour week is ~113 M subscription tokens out of the same pool as
your chat. No consumer-plan token ceiling is published by Anthropic, so I will not invent one;
divide your own Settings → Usage bar by **84.8 pages per million** at the production rate.

## §7 — What I would do next, given these numbers

1. **Stop sizing TikTok email work off 10.6%.** On your own marks it is zero. Either accept
   TikTok as a page source that does not yield emails, or find a contact route that is not the
   bio — the payload has no business field and no contact button, so this is a platform gap,
   not something a better parser fixes.
2. **Measure P(email | your WANT) on Instagram.** It has never been done. Every Instagram email
   figure sits on a filter-passed denominator, and TikTok showed that substitution costs 4.1×
   in the flattering direction. Agent B prices the closing experiment at about **$0.12**.
3. **Time the judging configuration that actually ships.** Every hours figure here rests on a
   rate measured on a configuration that was replaced.
4. **Use 12,393 tokens/page for planning, not 8,548.** The lab figure understates the plan draw
   by 45%.
5. **Protect the stock.** Working stock is 3× cheaper than new supply on every axis. When it is
   exhausted, hours per 1,000 wanted go from 15 to 46 in one step.
