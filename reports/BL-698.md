# BL-698 — Identifying the film or show from a clip: two of the three routes are structurally dead, the third is unmeasured, and the payoff chain you already assumed is licence-blocked

## AUDIO FINGERPRINTING CANNOT DO THIS AT ANY PRICE — ACRCloud's film/TV recognition matches against content **you** upload, so identifying an arbitrary film would require first possessing every film. Reverse image search on a **meme-composited** frame keys on other reposts of the same meme, not the source, and costs **$3.50/1,000** to do it. Dialogue→subtitle retrieval is the only route with a path to ~$0, and its blocker is not Whisper's garbled entities — it is that **nobody has measured how many of these clips contain film dialogue at all** (BL-682's "it is dialogue" rests on **n=6**, not its n=108 headline). Meanwhile the payoff chain is broken independently of all three: **TMDB forbids commercial use** (~$149/mo to license) and **OMDb is CC BY-NC — unusable outright**. Both should be replaced by **Wikidata (CC0)**, which this round verified holds **231,821 films with genre** and **272,702 with IMDb IDs**. **The cheapest next step is not a build — it is a $0 experiment that the 37.5% templated accounts already provide free ground truth for.**

**2026-07-29 · DEEP RESEARCH, read-only. No code written, no pipeline file changed, $0 spent — no paid API calls. Only public documentation, public pricing pages, and one free Wikidata SPARQL query.**

## PART 0 — the scope in the brief is right in spirit and wrong in shape

The brief scopes this research to "the OTHER ~62%". Two corrections change what that population *is*.

**It is 62.5% of ACCOUNTS, not of posts.** BL-692 measured caption templating as **all-or-nothing per account**, confirmed at 10× BL-682's n: an account either templates ~every post or none of them. So the target is not scattered untitled posts across all pages — it is **the entire output of the pages that never template**. There is no per-account bootstrap to exploit: on those pages, every post starts from zero.

**The residual is probably larger than 62.5%.** BL-692's 37.5% carries a CI of **21–57%**, so the residual is **43–79%**. And 50% of the templated accounts share a caption generator, so the effective n behind that figure is smaller than 24. On the raw discovery pool rather than qualified leads, templating drops to **~14%** — a residual of ~86%.

**Both numbers should be carried, because they answer different questions.** For "what fraction of leads I keep can I title for free", 37.5%. For "what fraction of everything I crawl", ~14%.

## PART 1 — dialogue → subtitle search

### There is no commercially-usable hosted quote→title API. The most obvious candidate answers the inverse question.

**VERIFIED — the OpenSubtitles API does not search subtitle text.** Its search endpoint takes `query` (a *title*), `imdb_id`, `moviehash`, `season`/`episode`, `year`. There is no parameter that accepts a line of dialogue. **It answers "which subtitle file corresponds to this known title" — precisely the inverse of the question here.** Anyone budgeting OpenSubtitles for this task is budgeting the wrong product. (Free tier 5–20 downloads/day; Basic 15,000/24h; full pricing behind a login — **GAP**.)

**UNVERIFIED LEAD — quote engines exist, but as consumer web products.** QuoDB, Playphrase, Subzin, Musely, AIMovieFinder all do fuzzy quote→title search; Musely advertises 18M subtitle/script lines across 320,000+ titles. **None publishes an API, pricing, or licence.** QuoDB's site exposes no API documentation. None is safe to depend on.

### That leaves a self-hosted index — and its licence is a GAP, not a green light

The OPUS OpenSubtitles corpus is **3,735,070 files, 3.35bn sentence fragments, 22.1bn tokens, 62 languages**. Its stated terms are only: *"Please, add a link to http://www.opensubtitles.org/ to your website and to your reports and publications produced with the data!"*

**Attribution is requested; commercial use is neither granted nor refused.** That is an absence of a licence, not a permissive one. And there is a second problem no corpus licence resolves: **the underlying film dialogue is itself copyrighted work.** A commercial full-text index over the dialogue of every film carries a risk that is legal, not technical, and it should be priced as such before anything is built.

### The interesting part: BL-682's veto is narrower than it reads

BL-682 concluded *"do not use Whisper output to identify which film a clip is from"*, on the evidence of `"Zoro is your undogsus"`. **That verdict is correct for the operation it tested — reading the title or character name directly off the transcript.**

**Retrieval is a different operation.** A distinctive word *sequence* can rank the right film first even with every proper noun mangled, because the signal lives in the n-gram pattern, not the entities. *"I'm going to make him an offer he can't refuse"* identifies a film uniquely and contains no proper noun at all. `"Zoro is your undogsus"` is worthless as a name and still serviceable as a query string.

**So the veto does not automatically extend to fuzzy retrieval.** It has simply never been tested — see PART 6, which is the whole recommendation.

### The real constraint is upstream, and it is unmeasured

**GAP, and it is the one that matters:** BL-682's finding that Whisper is transcribing *"movie and TV dialogue"* rather than lyrics was measured on **6 clip audios**, not the 108 in its headline. Meme pages routinely overlay trending music on a muted clip. **If the audio is music, there is no dialogue to retrieve and this entire route returns nothing for that clip** — no amount of index quality fixes it.

**How often that happens is the single number that bounds option 1, and no round has measured it.**

**GAP:** no published benchmark exists for "identify a film from noisy ASR against a global subtitle index." The adjacent literature covers ASR↔subtitle *alignment* (systems under 15% WER; subtitles themselves paraphrased and time-shifted) — related, but not this task.

## PART 2 — audio fingerprinting is structurally dead, and pricing is not why

**VERIFIED.** ACRCloud's film and TV capability is **"Custom Content" recognition**: their documentation describes recognising *"your own songs, advertisements, TV shows/series or movies"*, where developers *"upload audio/video files of content to ACRCloud buckets as identification samples."*

**There is no pre-built film or TV back-catalogue to match an arbitrary clip against.** To identify an arbitrary film, you would first have to ingest every film — which requires possessing every film. **The service cannot answer "what is this clip from". It answers "is this one of the things I already gave you."** Those are different products, and the marketing language ("TV and film content recognition") reads like the former while delivering the latter.

Their broadcast-monitoring product matches against **live channels they monitor**, not a back-catalogue — again a different question.

Pricing is therefore moot, but for the record: the only figure locatable is **$5.80/1,000 requests**, from a **2020 forum post** (**UNVERIFIED LEAD**; current pricing sits behind a login — **GAP**). At that rate 1,000 clips/day would run ~$174/month for something that structurally cannot do the job. **The 15–90s clip-length question in the brief never arises**; duration is not the limiting factor.

## PART 3 — frames: expensive, and pointed at the wrong nearest neighbour

| option | cost | verdict |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Google Vision Web Detection** | **$3.50 / 1,000** (first 1,000/mo free; **no volume discount published**) | The most expensive Vision feature — more than double Label or Logo detection. Returns matching web pages and best-guess labels, **not a structured film title** |
| **TinEye API** | **from $200/month** | Near-duplicate matching; its real strength is finding the *earliest* upload. Not a film index |
| **Google Images / Lens** | free | **No API, no batch processing. REFUTED** as a pipeline component |
| **trace.moe** | **free** — 1,000/mo, 120 req/min | Genuinely free, returns title + episode + exact timestamp. **Anime only** |

**The structural objection is specific to this corpus.** These are **meme-composited** frames: a caption bar burned over the image, cropped to 9:16, re-encoded. Reverse image search keys on near-duplicate pixels — and **the nearest duplicates of a meme frame are other reposts of the same meme, not the source film.** The technique is being asked to jump from a derivative to its origin, which is the one thing near-duplicate matching is worst at.

The brief correctly rules out SigLIP (a classifier, no subject identity). **Nothing above repairs that**; they are retrieval systems over web-crawled images, not scene databases.

**trace.moe is the one genuinely free win, and its value is unquantifiable today. GAP: the anime share of this corpus has never been measured — the word "anime" does not appear in any report in this programme.** If anime is a meaningful slice of the meme-page corpus, this is free titles for that slice; if it is 2%, it is noise. **That is a $0 measurement against captions already held.**

## PART 4 — the caption route, restated as the baseline to beat

Free, already working, **37.5%** of confirmed repost pages (CI 21–57%), **~14%** of the raw pool. Fields observed: `Title:` `Genre:` `Cast:` `Created by:` `Language:` `Release Date:` `Runtime:`. BL-682 measured `Title:` on 33/108 captions and `Genre:` on 34/108.

**Two properties make this more valuable than its hit rate suggests.** First, the genres it yields are **content** genres — Comedy 17, Drama 15, Sitcom 11, Fantasy 7, Action 7 — which is what a clip library indexes on. Second, and central to PART 6: **it is free labelled ground truth.**

## PART 5 — the payoff chain is licence-blocked, and this is the third time

The brief asks whether TMDB and OMDb are "free at this volume". **Volume is not the problem. Both forbid the commercial use this pipeline is.**

| service | terms | verdict |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **TMDB** | verbatim: *"The license…**does not permit any commercial use** of TMDB, the TMDB APIs, or TMDB Content."* Also forbids caching *"for longer than 6 months"*, and requires the TMDB logo plus a non-endorsement notice | **PAID.** Commercial licence reported at **~$149/month** under $1M revenue (**UNVERIFIED LEAD** — secondary source; not stated in the ToS itself). Rate limit ~40 req/s at IP level, identical for free and commercial keys |
| **OMDb** | **CC BY-NC 4.0** | **UNUSABLE.** Not a pricing question — the NC term excludes commercial use outright, at any tier |
| **IMDb datasets** | CC BY-NC-SA | **UNUSABLE**, same reason |

**This is the third licence wall in the same programme**, and the pattern deserves a name: BL-679 hit it on madmom (non-commercial models) and Essentia (AGPLv3/non-commercial); BL-682 hit it on MusicBrainz genre associations (CC BY-NC-SA). **The recurring failure is assuming "free to call" means "free to use commercially."** It is worth checking licence *before* coverage on every future data source in this programme.

### The clean replacement, verified this round

**Wikidata is CC0** — a public-domain dedication: no attribution requirement, no NC clause, no cache limit, and a full dump can be held locally so there is no per-call cost and no rate limit at all.

**VERIFIED by live SPARQL query against `query.wikidata.org`:**

| query | result |
| --- | --- |
| films (`wdt:P31 wd:Q11424`) with a genre statement (`wdt:P136`) | **231,821** |
| films with an IMDb ID (`wdt:P345`) | **272,702** |
| TV series (`wdt:P31 wd:Q5398426`) with a genre statement | **44,276** |

That is ample for the mainstream, heavily-memed catalogue these pages draw on, and the IMDb-ID coverage means a title resolved by any route can be joined to Wikidata directly. **Re-point the payoff chain at Wikidata regardless of which identification route is chosen** — this is the one recommendation in this report that is unconditional, free, and can be done today.

## PART 6 — verdict

### The honest answer to "if nothing works below a price I'd hate, say so"

**Two of the three routes do not work at any price**, and that is a stronger statement than "too expensive":

* **Audio fingerprinting** cannot identify content it has not been given. Dead structurally.
* **Frames** cost $3.50/1,000 to be pointed at the wrong nearest neighbour on composited memes. Dead on accuracy before cost matters. (Except anime, free, share unknown.)

**Dialogue→subtitle retrieval is the only route with a path to ~$0** — Whisper is already measured at **~1.6s CPU per 30s clip** and a self-hosted index has no per-call cost. But it carries **three unquantified risks**: an unclear corpus licence over copyrighted dialogue, an unmeasured share of clips that contain no dialogue at all, and an unmeasured retrieval accuracy on garbled ASR.

**So: no, there is no cheap reliable answer available today.** What there is, is a cheap way to find out — and it has not been run.

### The cheapest next step is a $0 experiment, not a build

**The 37.5% of accounts that template their captions are free labelled ground truth for the 62.5% that do not.** Those posts carry a `Title:` field *and* the audio. Nobody has used them as an evaluation set.

The experiment, entirely on data already held, with no paid call and no index build:

1. Take the templated posts where `Title:` is present — a labelled set at zero cost.
2. Run Whisper `tiny` (already installed, ~1.6s/30s) over their audio.
3. **First, measure the number that bounds everything: what fraction produce real dialogue at all** rather than music or silence? BL-682's `no_speech_prob > 0.5` rule already separates cleanly, and this closes the n=6 gap.
4. For those that do, test retrieval of the known title from the garbled transcript.

**Step 3 alone is decisive.** If most clips carry no dialogue, option 1 dies without anyone downloading a 22-billion-token corpus or resolving its licence. If most do, step 4 gives a real hit rate on real garbled output against real labels — the number this brief actually wants, and the number no amount of further desk research can produce.

### What to do regardless of that outcome

1. **Re-point the payoff chain from TMDB/OMDb to Wikidata (CC0) now.** Free, unrestricted, verified coverage. This is independent of everything else here.
2. **Keep the caption parser as the primary source** and treat any identification route as a supplement to it, never a replacement.
3. **Measure the anime share** from captions already held. If it is material, trace.moe is free titles.
4. **Check licence before coverage** on every future data source in this programme. Three walls in three rounds is a process problem, not bad luck.

**Nothing was built, no pipeline file was changed, and no paid call was made. No key appears in this document.**
