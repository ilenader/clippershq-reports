## Coverage first — the number that decides whether this is worth wiring

Measured on the real BL-702 corpus, `scratch/bl701/discovered.json`, **n = 351 reels across
317 accounts**:

| source | clips | share |
|---|---|---|
| **hashtag-derived genre** | **321** | **91.5%** |
| caption-declared genre | 10 | 2.8% |
| both tiers present | 10 | 2.8% |
| **either (any genre at all)** | **321** | **91.5%** |
| neither | 30 | 8.5% |
| **franchise identified** | **207** | **59.0%** |
| declared/derived conflicts | 4 | 1.1% |

**The caption adds exactly zero clips beyond the tag: 321 either vs 321 tag-derived.** Every
one of the 10 declared rows also had a tag genre. On this corpus the caption tier is not a
coverage mechanism at all — it is a *precision* mechanism, upgrading 10 clips from an
inferred label to the account's own stated one.

**All 30 uncovered clips are #capcut** — and that is by design, not a gap. #capcut names an
editing tool, not a genre. The map returns nothing for it on purpose.

**59% get a named franchise**, which is the more valuable half of the result: Marvel
Cinematic Universe 57, One Piece 30, Naruto 30, Dragon Ball Z 30, The Boys 30, Batman 30.
Knowing a clip is *Batman* is strictly more useful to an editing bot than knowing it is
*action* — the genre is derivable from the franchise, never the reverse.

## The tag bank — and a correction to the brief

The brief said BL-692 lists the tag bank. **It does not.** BL-692 is about caption templates
and sticky tags; it names only six hashtags in passing (#batman, #dccomics, #90s, #nostalgia,
#childhood, #cartoon) and contains no canonical list.

The real bank is in code: `repost_finder.MEASURED_TAG_HIT_RATE` (**32 tags**, a strict
superset of `DEFAULT_HASHTAGS`'s 24). Five further tags used by the BL-702 discovery corpus
sit outside it: `anime`, `animeedit`, `capcut`, `dragonballz`, `marveledit`.

**All 37 are mapped**, and the test asserts full bank coverage against the live constant, so
the map cannot silently drift when someone adds a tag.

Franchise recorded wherever the tag names a property — including aliases: #brucewayne and
#gothamcity both resolve to **Batman**, #marvelmovie / #marveledit / #mcu all to **Marvel
Cinematic Universe**.

**Five tags map to no genre on purpose**: `capcut`, `pov`, `trend`, `movie`, `series`. They
name a tool, a format, or a medium. Emitting a confident genre for them would be exactly the
failure this module exists to avoid.

## Declared outranks derived

`content_genre` resolves to the caption-declared list whenever the account declared one, and
falls back to the tag inference otherwise. `content_genre_source` says which tier won.

The derived list is **never discarded** — it stays in `genre_derived` regardless. A consumer
can always audit what the tag would have said.

Provenance follows the existing ladder from `caption_parser` (that module's constants and its
`parse_template_fields` are imported rather than restated):

| field | tier |
|---|---|
| `content_genre` | `declared` when the caption declared, else `derived`, else `absent` |
| `genre_declared` | `declared` / `absent` |
| `genre_derived` | `derived` / `absent` |
| `franchise` | `derived` — it is an inference from the tag, never a declaration |

`field()` enforces the consumer rule: a field whose provenance is `absent`, or which has no
provenance entry at all, reads as **None**. An unattributable value cannot leak downstream.

## Disagreements are flagged, not resolved

When both tiers produce genres and the two sets are **disjoint**, `genre_conflict` is True
and both lists stay readable. One shared label counts as agreement — "Comedy, Sitcom" vs
"comedy" is the same claim, not a clash.

**4 conflicts fired on the corpus (1.1%), and all four are instructive:**

```
#anime      declared=[action, fantasy]                     derived=[anime]
#animeedit  declared=[action, fantasy]                     derived=[anime]
#animeedit  declared=[action, comedy, fantasy, superhero]  derived=[anime]
#animeedit  declared=[action, adventure, fantasy, romance] derived=[anime]
```

**These are arguably false positives, and I am reporting them as such rather than tuning them
away.** "Anime" is a *medium*, not a genre — it can never overlap with action or fantasy, so
a medium-vs-genre pairing trips the disjointness test every time even though the two
statements are perfectly compatible. A clip can be anime *and* action.

The honest reading: the conflict flag currently detects "the two tiers used different
vocabularies" as well as "the two tiers disagree." Separating medium from genre in the canon
would fix it. That is a real design gap, it is left in place rather than papered over, and
the flag still does its job on the case it was built for — a #batman clip declaring "Comedy"
is correctly flagged and both labels survive.

## Limits

- **Coverage is a property of the tag set, not of the module.** 91.5% holds because the
  BL-702 corpus was drawn from 12 mapped tags. A run on unmapped tags returns nothing —
  correctly, but the headline number would fall.
- **The caption tier is nearly empty here (2.8%)** because BL-694 established templating is
  per-account and all-or-nothing, and this corpus has 317 accounts with ~1 clip each. On a
  templated account's full catalogue the declared tier would carry far more weight. The 2.8%
  measures *this sampling shape*, not the value of the tier.
- **The genre for a franchise tag is the franchise's genre, not the clip's.** A comedic
  Batman edit tagged #batman still derives superhero/action/crime. That is what the declared
  tier is for, and what the conflict flag surfaces.
- **The conflict rule cannot yet tell "different vocabulary" from "genuine disagreement"**
  (see above). 4/4 observed conflicts are the benign kind.
- Nothing here is wired into the funnel. It is a pure function with no caller.
