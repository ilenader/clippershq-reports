## Defect 1 — we were rewriting your text. Fixed at the parameter.

**What happened.** `scratch/bl1128_render.py:157` passed

```python
caption=str(cl.field(rec, "caption") or "")[:200]
```

and `caption` on a clip record is **the reposter's Instagram caption** — the words they typed
into the post box. That is where "This cool night air is curious" came from. BL-1128's own
README printed the OCR'd line beside each render under the heading "the source's own burned-in
line", so the OCR was being read, reported, and then thrown away while a different string was
drawn. **The report and the pixels disagreed and only the pixels shipped.**

**Two separate faults, and the second one is why the OCR was not usable even if it had been
passed.** RapidOCR reads these frames essentially perfectly; the garbled readings in BL-1128
were assembly faults:

| | BL-1128 recorded | now | your own transcription |
|---|---|---|---|
| clip 03 | `Me a bro before and changed for a g he girl:` | `Me and bro before he changed for a girl:` | `Me and bro before he changed for a girl:` |
| clip 02 | `Me when bro's toxic e ex tries to get back with him:` | `Me when bro's toxic ex tries to get back with him:` | `Me when bro's toxic ex tries to get back with him:` |

Causes, both measured:

1. **The band crop fragments words.** BL-1128 cut the frame down to the text band and OCR'd
   that. RapidOCR wants context around a line; cut to the band it split "Me and" into
   `Me a` + `and`. On clip 03 the tight crop returns **7 blocks and garbles the line**; the
   **full frame returns 2 blocks and reads it exactly.**
2. **RapidOCR returns detection order, not reading order.** Clip 03's blocks came back
   `Me a`(x=150), `bro`(x=492), `before`(x=655), `and`(x=301) — and were joined in that order.

`clippershq/clip_label_text.py` OCRs the whole frame, keeps the blocks whose boxes fall inside
the band, and assembles them into lines by vertical overlap and words by x.

**THE ROOT FIX — the parameter is no longer a string.** `render_one(caption=...)` accepted any
string, which is why nothing refused the wrong one. It is now `render_one(label=...)` and takes
a `clip_label_text.SourceLine`, which only `read_source_line` can produce. **Passing the
scraped caption is now a `RewrittenLabel` exception at the call site**, not a defect on your
screen. `--label-verbatim` is passed unconditionally from that entry point, so the line cannot
be sent through `caption_headline` → `strip_emoji` → `caption_hook(90)` — every one of which
rewrites text, and any one of which would have turned a verbatim line into a different line
with every existing test still green.

**"If OCR cannot read it confidently, refuse."** Confidence is **cross-frame agreement**: the
burned-in line does not change between second 2 and second 8, so five frames are sampled and
**three must return the identical string, character for character**, with the weakest OCR block
clearing 0.80. Disagreement is per-clip evidence that the engine is failing on this material —
no vendor's self-reported score can tell you that. A refusal is a named reason, never an empty
string.

**The test you asked for:** `tests/test_label_text_verbatim.py`, 15 checks. It reads the
**textfile ffmpeg actually draws** and fails if it is not the input character for character;
it asserts the cleaner chain still rewrites (so the guard cannot quietly become decoration);
and the verifier goes further — it **re-OCRs our own rendered pixels** and compares. 3 of 3
identical.

One consequence worth naming: an emoji in your source line is now a **refusal**, not a strip.
Montserrat has no emoji glyphs, so drawing `bad😭` verbatim would put a `□` where your
character was, and a box is not your character.

## Defect 2 — the label geometry. All four notes are one measurement.

Your three complaints — touch the video, hide their text completely, give the text padding and
make the strip taller — were the same miss. `measure_label_box` returned the **ink's** band.
But your sources paint their text on a **card**: a flat panel running from the top of the frame
down to where the footage begins. Covering the ink leaves the rest of that card showing above
and below, in a slightly different tone. On clip 02 that was **90 px above and 33 px below** —
the seam you photographed, measured.

`clip_pipeline.measure_label_card` finds the card instead, and the property it uses is that
**the card does not move and the picture does**. Per-row standard deviation across time, at
full vertical resolution. On your own six sources, card rows measure **0.0–0.4** and the first
picture row jumps to **4.7–31.2** — an order of magnitude, not a tuned threshold.

The strip's bottom edge is therefore the picture's first moving row: there is no gap available
to photograph. Its top is the top of the frame. Their text is inside it by construction,
descenders and underlines included. And because the card is taller than the ink ever was,
there is room for the padding.

Measured on the three shipped renders, off the delivered frames:

| | strip | padding above / below | drawn type | source's own type |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 01 | 1080×429, x=0, y=0 | 193 px / 193 px | 43 px | 44 px |
| 02 | 1080×303, x=0, y=0 | 133 px / 133 px | 37 px | 39 px |
| 03 | 720×329, x=0, y=0 | 115 px / 115 px | 49 px | 48 px |

**The type matches your source's own size**, which is what you asked for — "you can just make
the same size as the original". It is measured, not chosen: `read_source_line` returns the
pixel height of your line from the OCR block box drawn around your actual glyphs, and the
fitter searches for the point size whose drawn ink height matches it. BL-1128 went the other
way and made type 2.4× larger on the theory that bigger was the complaint.

**A clip with no static card is now REFUSED.** Your clip 01 — the Justice League reaction, text
on a pill over live footage — has no card, and nothing we draw can hide that text cleanly.
`tests/test_label_geometry.py` (11 checks) proves all of this against a synthetic source whose
card boundary is known in advance.

## Defect 5 — the originals. Diagnosed, and clip 01 is the proof.

| | video | audio | double-clicks |
|---|---|---|---|
| `01_smoovereactss__ORIGINAL` | **h264/High** | aac **xHE-AAC** | **NO** |
| 02, 03, 05 | AV1/Main | aac xHE-AAC | NO |
| 04, 06 | VP9 | aac xHE-AAC | NO |
| every `__OURS` | h264/High | aac **LC** | yes |

**Clip 01 is the whole diagnosis.** Its video is already h264 — the codec Windows has decoded
natively since Windows 7 — and it still would not open. What all six share, and no `OURS` has,
is **xHE-AAC audio** (MPEG-D USAC under an `mp4a` tag). Windows' built-in AAC decoder handles
LC and HE, not xHE-AAC, so the default player cannot build a playback topology and falls back
to "choose an app". AV1/VP9 is a **second, independent** cause on the other five. Fixing only
the video would have left clip 01 exactly as broken while looking like a fix.

`operator_pairs.playability` reproduces your report exactly: **6 of 6 originals fail, 6 of 6
ours pass.** `make_windows_playable` rewrites them — video **copied** when already h264 (a true
remux, bit-identical picture), transcoded at CRF 16 only for AV1/VP9; audio always re-encoded,
because there is no lossless path out of xHE-AAC. `+faststart`. The output is **re-probed after
writing**, so the claim is about the shipped file. All three originals in the new folder were
AV1/VP9 and were transcoded; that is named in the README rather than hidden.

**The text is the reliable mood signal, and this round is the argument for that.** It is the
