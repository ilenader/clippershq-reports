## The answer in one paragraph

The wall is **not** about cookies, sessions, browser fingerprints or how fast we go. It is an
**IP-level decision, taken before a single byte of JavaScript runs**, and what triggers it is
**how many account pages one IP asks for**. Every clean run in the record was **135 pages or
fewer** — including a 6-lane run at 34 pages a minute, *faster* than the run that died. The run
that died asked for **1,200**. It has not reset in **3.1 hours** of continuous probing, and I
cannot tell you when it will. None of that matters as much as this: **the paid replacement is
an endpoint the codebase already calls, it costs $0.0006 a page, and a whole 1,200-page run
costs 72 cents.**

## 2. What its scope is — IP, before fingerprint

Three independent instruments, all pointed at the same pages, all agreeing:

| instrument | result |
|---|---|
| plain HTTP GET, **no cookies, no browser at all** | **34 of 34 walled** |
| Playwright with a **brand-new context per page** (zero shared state) | **34 of 34 walled** |
| Scrapling's stealth fetcher, **5 different engines/levers** | **8 of 8 walled, at request 1** |

A fresh context carries no cookie and no session, and it is walled exactly like everything
else. The redirect to `/accounts/login/?next=…&is_from_rle` arrives as a **302 before any
JavaScript executes**, byte-identical whether the client is curl, our Playwright, or a
purpose-built stealth browser. **A wall that fires before the fingerprint is presented cannot
be a fingerprint problem** — which is also why no amount of stealth engineering will move it.

**It is scoped to account pages, not to Instagram.** Measured just now:

| surface | redirected to login? |
|---|---|
| `instagram.com/<handle>/` | **yes** |
| `instagram.com/<handle>/reels/` | **yes** |
| `instagram.com/explore/tags/memes/` | no |
| `instagram.com/` | no |

That looked like a door — it is not. Both non-redirecting URLs return **the same 606 KB empty
app shell with zero post permalinks**. They do not redirect *and* they carry no data. **There
is no free surface left.**

## 6. Free versus paid — the decision

`known_user_pks()` already skips the resolve when a `user_pk` is on hand, and FACTS §3 already
books a profile resolve at **1.062 billed calls per Instagram page**. So the resolve is
*already bought*, and replacing capture is **one extra call**:

| wiring | + per page | $ / page | **$ / 1,000 pages** | $ / 1,000 wanted |
|---|---:|---:|---:|---:|
| capture stays free — *and returns 0 pages* | $0.0000 | $0.001226 | $1.23 | $4.38 |
| **paid capture, pk reused (1 call)** | **$0.0006** | **$0.001826** | **$1.83** | **$6.52** |
| paid capture, pk re-resolved (2 calls) | $0.0012 | $0.002426 | $2.43 | $8.66 |

*(at his real 28.0% Instagram want rate — 3,571 pages walked per 1,000 wanted.)*

Now the two numbers that end the argument:

- **Paid capture is 1.5% of what it costs to judge the same page** on the vision API
  ($0.0006 against $0.040932). On the free sub-agent path it is the *only* cost in the pipeline
  — and still under two dollars a thousand.
- **Buying block B outright — the 524 pages that returned absolutely nothing — costs $0.31.
  Buying the entire 1,200-page run costs $0.72.**

**Buy it.** Not because free capture is expensive, but because it now returns zero, and the
thing that replaces it costs less than a cup of coffee per hundred thousand pages. The
engineering is a stage that turns a medias envelope into a montage; the client call already
ships.

`user_pk` is currently persisted on only **221 of 62,692** master rows. Persisting the pk we
already pay to resolve is what keeps this at one call instead of two — a field we receive and
throw away.

**I have written no product code for this.** The wiring is a real change to the capture stage
and it belongs in its own round with its own guards.

## 8. Spend — and a leak I hit myself

`spend.json` showed **none** of this round's 40 calls. The cause is exact:
`_maybe_autoflush` only runs **during a billed call**, and its thresholds are 25 calls or 60
seconds. A client that makes its paid calls up front and then does slow free work (fetching
images) **exits owing the debt no matter how long it ran**, because no later billed call ever
arrives to trigger the staleness check.

I flushed it deliberately and attributed it:

| | calls | $ |
|---|---:|---:|
| `bl1334_paid_grid` (mine, 4 invocations) | 16 | $0.0096 |
| `bl1334_endpoint_sweep` (sub-agent A) | 24 | $0.0144 |
| **total** | **40** | **$0.0240** |

Ledger `ig_spent_usd` **$21.492282 → $21.516282**, delta **$0.024000**, agreeing to the cent
with 40 × $0.0006. Counted as **attempts** — 18 × 200, 3 × 400, 1 × 404 among the sweep, and an
empty or failed result is still billed.

**This is a real defect and it is not this round's to fix**, but any short-lived paid script
in this codebase is silently losing its spend the same way.

## In plain language

Instagram has not blocked our browser, our cookies, or our disguise. It has made a decision
about **this internet connection**, and it made it because we asked for 1,200 accounts in one
go when nothing before had asked for more than 135. The decision is taken before the page even

in over three hours and I genuinely don't know when it will.

The good news is much bigger than the bad news. The company we already buy Instagram data from
will simply **sell** us the pictures — the same grid, the same burned-in captions the judge
reads, at better quality than a screenshot — for **six hundredths of a cent a page**. The
entire run that failed would have cost **72 cents**. The endpoint is one we already have
configured and already call from our own code, and the same call throws in the view counts we
were told Instagram never gives us.

I have not wired it in. That is a proper change with proper tests, and it deserves its own
round.
