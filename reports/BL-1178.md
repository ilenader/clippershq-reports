# BL-1178 — discovery was never the constraint: 6,681 pages for $0.15, and OCR is what binds

**33 seeds produced 6,681 distinct handles in 242 billed calls — $0.1452, six and a half
minutes.** That is 27.5 new handles per call. The previous round found 62 pages in two hours,
and the reason was never supply: the system can discover meme pages roughly **700 times faster
than it can judge them**.

The binding constraint, measured: **10.9 seconds of OCR per page versus 2.3 seconds of API
budget.** Reading the burned-in text is free in money and expensive in clock, and it is now
the whole cost.

Every figure here came from **`.venv\Scripts\python.exe`, Python 3.11.9**. Bare `python` is
3.13.14 and lacks the packages; it was used only for the stdlib-only claim readers.

### THE THIRD TIME A NESTED PAYLOAD HAS COST THIS PROJECT MONEY

`/v2/user/following` and `/v2/user/followers` return

```
{"next_page_id": "...", "response": {"users": [ ... ]}}
```

— the users are **one level down**. A reader that inspects only the top level sees a two-key
dict and reports an empty path. **That is why "who a meme page follows" looked unavailable
when the operator could see it working in his own browser.** He was right and the instrument
was wrong.

The same shape has now cost: 117 billed requests judging twelve empty captions each
(`{"media": {...}}`), 115 already-paid pages stranded (a checkpoint row read off the envelope
instead of `payload`), and this. `_users_of()` is written once and every path goes through it;
`tests/test_meme_finder.py` pins all four wrapper shapes.

**Keyword search exists, exactly as he described.** `/v2/fbsearch/accounts` returned 19–20
accounts for every plain-English query — "meme page", "daily memes", "funny reels" — and 150
of 155 were new.

**No path exists for "accounts that repost the same clip".** `/v2/music/medias` returns null
and there is no reposter-by-media endpoint. `/v1/media/likers` is the nearest thing and it is
a different question: a liker is an account that *liked* a meme, not one that *posts* them.

## 3. Yield inverts the ranking

A path returning 500 handles of which 5 pass is worse than one returning 50 that all pass. So
a stratified sample of 12 per path was judged with the real filter.

| path | judged | passed | rate | 95% Wilson | new/call | **$ per PASSING page** |
|---|---:|---:|---:|---|---:|---:|
| **following** | 12 | 2 | **16.7%** | [4.7, 44.8] | 8.7 | **$0.0040** |
| search | 12 | 1 | 8.3% | [1.5, 35.4] | 18.8 | $0.0076 |
| followers | 12 | 0 | 0.0% | [0.0, 24.3] | 20.0 | n/a |
| suggested | 12 | 0 | 0.0% | [0.0, 24.3] | 54.6 | n/a |

**`suggested` has the most volume and the worst yield; `following` has the least volume and
the best.** That is the operator's own observation — "when I go to his following, I can see a
lot of the pages we're looking for" — and it is the reverse of what volume alone would tell
you.

**Stated honestly: at n=12 these intervals overlap and following cannot be separated from
suggested.** What *is* established is that **no path is anywhere near a majority** — overall
**3 of 48 = 6.2% [2.1, 16.8]**. The ranking is a lead worth following, not a proven order.

## 4. The real ceiling

At the measured **0.44 req/s** limiter, one hour buys 1,584 billed calls = **$0.95/hour** of
vendor spend. Spent entirely on discovery that is **43,514 new handles per hour**. Spent
entirely on judging it is 1,584 pages — *if the API were the constraint.*

It is not:

| per page judged | seconds |
|---|---:|
| API budget at 0.44 req/s | 2.3 |
| **OCR** (73% of pages survive the free gates and get a video downloaded) | **10.9** |

**OCR binds, by 4.7x.** Real throughput: **328 pages judged/hour → 20.5 passing pages/hour.**
That matches the previous round exactly — two hours produced 62 passing pages.

**To work the 6,681 handles already discovered: 20.3 hours, $4.26, ~417 passing pages
[140, 1,122].**

| budget | pages judged | passing | wall clock |
|---:|---:|---:|---:|
| $3.00 | 4,705 | ~294 | 14.3 h |
| $10.00 | 15,686 | ~980 | 47.7 h |
| $50.00 | 78,431 | ~4,901 | 238.6 h |

**So the answer to "why only 62 when there are millions" is: nothing is stopping the discovery
of millions — 43,514/hour is reachable today. What costs is looking at each one.** Money runs
out at roughly $1/hour; the clock runs out at 20.5 usable pages/hour. To go faster the OCR leg
has to get cheaper — fewer frames, one post instead of two, or the post's own thumbnail
instead of the downloaded video. That is the next lever and it is not a money problem either.

### Spend — receipt-backed, and an attribution trap

| label | vendor receipts | $ |
|---|---:|---:|
| `bl1178_probe` | 19 | 0.0114 |
| `bl1178_probe2` | 4 | 0.0024 |
| `bl1178_crawl` | 247 | 0.1482 |
| **this round** | **270** | **$0.1620** |

**$0.1620 of a $3.00 cap.** My governor reserved 290 attempts and the ledger recorded 270
receipts; the gap is calls reserved before a request that then failed to leave. Attempts is
the number I capped on, receipts is the number billed, and the smaller one is not the one to
budget with.

> **`spend.json` also holds `bl1178_pull`, 175 calls, $0.1050 — THAT IS NOT MINE.** I never
> used that label. BL-1179 is writing under this round's filename prefix (it claims
> `scratch/bl1178_gate.py`). Summing the ledger by prefix would have charged this round
> $0.1050 of another round's money. Ledger attribution is by LABEL, and a label is only as
> good as the round that sets it.
