# BL-1277 — batching did not hurt accuracy on this test, and the saving is 7.0x

**Quick paired test. $0.00 vendor spend** — both arms judged already-captured 560×560 grids,
nothing was fetched, and the claim never listed `spend.json`. Every number was produced by
`.venv\Scripts\python.exe` (3.11.9) by full path; `python` on PATH is 3.13, lacks the
project's packages, and produced nothing here.

**The answer:**

| | 1 page per agent | 8 pages per agent |
|---|---:|---:|
| agreement with his marks | 22/24 = **91.7%** [74.2, 97.7] | 23/24 = **95.8%** [79.8, 99.3] |
| on his WANTs | 7/8 = 87.5% [52.9, 97.8] | **8/8 = 100%** [67.6, 100.0] |
| on his REJECTs | 15/16 = 93.8% [71.7, 98.9] | 15/16 = 93.8% [71.7, 98.9] |
| **tokens per page** | **59,759** | **8,548** |
| pages per million | 16.7 | **117.0** |

**Batching did not hurt. The two arms disagreed on exactly one page of 24, and on that page
the batched arm was the one that got it right.** The saving is **6.99×**, slightly better than
the 6.4× claimed.

**But read the intervals before acting.** n=24. The two arms' agreement intervals overlap
almost completely, and the single discordant pair gives an exact two-sided p of **1.0**. This
test can rule out a *gross* accuracy collapse. It cannot rule out a few-point drift, and it is
not evidence that none exists.

---

## 1. How the test was built

**His marks, and the label derived not read.** `mark` in this project means *"the judge's
verdict was correct"*, not *"this page is good"*, so the label is
`he_wants = (judge_verdict == "WANT") XOR (mark == "BAD")`. Reading `mark` directly is the
error that produces "18 of 100" when his true WANTs are 19.

**Same pages, same order, same brief, both arms.** The only difference is how many pages one
agent holds. Each agent had its own output directory.

**Contamination: 0 pages excluded, and that is measured.** All 100 handles were matched
against each candidate brief with a strict word-boundary test:

| brief | leaks against his 100 |
|---|---:|
| **`bl1242_brief_v4.md` — used here** | **0** |
| `bl1262_brief_v7.md` | 6 (`aboutfacts_786`, `acog.hitbox`, `cultr.ent`, `filmoviment`, `maestremoros`, `thedunkcentralhq`) — **not used** |
| `bl1265_brief_v8.md` | 0 |

*Correction to the premise I was given:* the "15 scored pages" leak does not hold for v4
against this set — v4 names none of them. The v8 hit reported by a naive substring scan was
the common word **"drop"** and is a false positive. Using v4 means **no page had to be
excluded**, so n stayed at 24 rather than shrinking.

**Stratified 8 WANT / 16 REJECT, deliberately.** The natural base rate is 19%, which would put
4–5 WANTs in 24 pages and leave the clustering test nothing to detect. The consequence is
stated rather than hidden: **overall agreement here is not the population figure**, which is
why per-class agreement leads the table above. Batch WANT counts were [3, 3, 2] — even by
construction, so clustering could not be manufactured by the sampling.

## 2. The paired comparison — one page, named

The arms agree on **23 of 24 = 95.8%** [79.8, 99.3].

| page | his label | 1-per-agent | 8-per-agent | batch, position |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| `filmoviment` | **WANT** | REJECT ✗ | **WANT ✓** | batch 2, position 2 |

**Discordant pairs: 0 favouring the single arm, 1 favouring the batched arm.** Exact two-sided
sign test **p = 1.0**. With one discordant pair no split could reach significance — the test
is reported so its weakness is visible, not to claim a null.

The direction is worth one sentence, and only one: the page batching got right and isolation
got wrong is one his marks say he *wants*. That is the opposite of the failure mode we were
looking for, on a sample of one.

---

## 3. The specific failure we were hunting: did verdicts anchor?

A score that holds while verdicts anchor is still a failure, so the score is not the test.

| | measured |
|---|---|
| WANTs called per batch | **[4, 3, 2]** against true [3, 3, 2] |
| variance of per-batch counts | 0.667 against a binomial expectation of 1.875 — **under**dispersed, and anchoring produces **over**dispersion |
| adjacent pairs given the same verdict | 7/21 = **33.3%** [17.2, 54.6] |
| **the truth's own adjacency in the same order** | **33.3%** |

**The judge's adjacent-same rate equals the truth's exactly.** Anchoring would push it
*above* — a batched judge that drifts toward its neighbours produces runs. There is no such
signal here.

**The control matters and I got it wrong the first time.** The obvious baseline —
p² + (1−p)² = 53.1% for a random order — is the wrong one, because this sample was
*interleaved on purpose*, so adjacent pages mostly differ by construction and any accurate
judge would score "below chance" and look anti-anchored for free. The honest control is what
the truth itself does in the same order, and against that the judge is exactly level.

**Power, stated plainly: three batches cannot rule out a moderate anchoring effect.** This is
absence of evidence. A gross effect — batches collapsing toward all-WANT or all-REJECT —
would have shown, and did not.

## 4. Tokens: 6.99×, and the marginal page is cheaper than expected

Measured from `subagent_tokens` on all 27 agents.

| | per agent | per page | n |
|---|---:|---:|---:|
| 1 page per agent | 59,759 | **59,759** | 24 |
| 8 pages per agent | 68,383 | **8,548** | 3 |

**6.99× — the 6.4× claim is confirmed and slightly beaten.**

Fitting `tokens_per_page(N) = fixed/N + marginal` to the two points:

- **fixed cost per agent: 58,527 tokens**
- **marginal cost of one more page inside an agent: 1,232 tokens**

The marginal page is *cheaper here than the 2,317 measured previously* — this brief is 21,140
chars against 28,282, the image is 560×560 (418 tokens) against 465×992 (615), and the reasons
were one to two sentences. So the marginal cost is a property of the configuration, not a
constant.

## 5. The ceiling, stated plainly — and one correction

**He wants 1,000 pages per million tokens. It is not reachable on this path.**

The asymptotic ceiling — batch size infinity, zero overhead, pure marginal cost — is
**811.7 pages per million**, because asking a model to look at a picture and answer costs
**1,232 tokens** and no batching removes that. His target sits **23% above the physical
floor**.

**The correction:** the figure I was given for that floor was ~315 pages per million, from a
per-page work block of 3,165.7 tokens. That measurement was TikTok's — a bigger brief, a
bigger image and longer reasons. On this Instagram configuration the marginal page is 1,232,
so the true asymptotic ceiling is **811.7, not 315**. The conclusion is unchanged and the
number is nearly 2.6× better than stated: **1,000/M remains out of reach, but by 23%, not by
3×.**

The practical figure is lower still. At a batch of 8 — the size actually tested — it is
**117 pages per million**. Reaching even 300/M needs a batch of ~32, which is four times
beyond anything whose accuracy has been measured.

---

## 6. Recommendation

**Take the batching. Use 8 pages per agent, and do not go further without re-testing.**

- 8 is the size tested. It cost nothing measurable in accuracy on n=24 and returned **7.0×**
  the pages per token.
- **Do not extrapolate to 16, 32 or 64 on this evidence.** The token model extrapolates
  cleanly; the *accuracy* does not, and anchoring risk grows with the number of pages sharing
  one context. Each step up needs its own test, and each is cheap.
- **Keep the per-agent output directory.** It costs nothing and closes a recorded hazard.
- The one page the arms disagreed on is worth an eye: `filmoviment` is a page he wants that
  the isolated arm rejected.

**What would make this conclusive:** the same paired design at n≈100 with batches of 8, and
arms at 8 vs 16. That is roughly 7.5 M subscription tokens at the 1-per-agent rate — about 4.5
judged pages' worth of *saving* per page tested, so it pays for itself immediately if the
answer holds.
