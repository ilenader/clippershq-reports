# BL-716 — what a sound is FOR, inferred from what it's used on: the idea works, the labeller is the ceiling

## THE HYPOTHESIS SURVIVED EVERY TEST IT COULD BE GIVEN, AND IT IS NOT ENOUGH YET. Of the sounds where the free labeller produced any verdict at all, **12 of 12 are concentrated — not one scattered sound in the sample.** Dragon Ball Z 100%, Naruto 100%, Breaking Bad 100%, Ryan Gosling 100%, Marvel 100%, One Piece 67%. That is exactly the signal the idea predicted, and it is the opposite of the singleton problem that killed BL-708's franchise-to-sound direction. **But only 4 of 27 sounds (15%) in the ≥1,000-clip band could be labelled at all**, on a median of 3 labelled clips out of 12. The blocker is not concentration and not the endpoint — it is that popular sounds are used by people who tag `#fyp` and `#viral`, not `#naruto`. **The direction is right; the labeller is the ceiling, and it is a fixable ceiling.**

## PART 0 — the envelope bug caught my own analysis script

The first pass reported **0 labels on all 43 sounds**. That was not a finding; it was BL-697's bug reproducing itself inside this round's code. `/v2/track/by/id` wraps every item as `{"media": {…}}`, exactly as `/v2/user/clips` does. My `caption_of()` read the wrapper, got a one-key dict, and returned `""` for all 452 clips — so every label came back empty and the result read as a clean, confident "sounds are not concentrated".

Unwrapping (`scratch/bl712_measure.py:_unwrap`) turned 0 labelled sounds into 13, from the **same bytes, at $0** — every payload was already saved. Everything below is the corrected pass.

Recording it because it is the third independent instance of this class, and the first where the victim was analysis code rather than the pipeline. **A shape mismatch here does not error — it produces a plausible negative result.** Had I not checked why the number was exactly zero, this report would have concluded "the idea does not work".

## PART 2 — why coverage is low, measured rather than assumed

452 clips inspected across all 41 sounds:

| | clips | share |
|---|---|---|
| caption **empty** | 17 | 4% |
| caption text, **no hashtag** | 128 | 28% |
| caption **with hashtag** | **307** | **68%** |

**Captions are not missing — 68% carry hashtags.** The tags are simply the wrong kind. The twelve most common across the whole sample:

```
fyp 36 · viral 35 · explore 22 · reels 20 · trending 19 · explorepage 15
trendingreels 12 · ryangosling 10 · spiderman 10 · viralreels 9
fyppppp… 9 · musafircafe 9
```

Ten of the twelve are reach-farming tags carrying no content signal. `content_genre` resolves franchise and medium from a curated tag table, so a clip tagged `#fyp #viral #trending` produces nothing — correctly, since those words say nothing about content.

**And the effect is strongest exactly where it hurts most:**

| band | has_hashtag | text_no_hashtag | empty |
|---|---|---|---|
| high-usage (≥1,000) | 205 | 92 | 13 |
| low-usage (<1,000) | 102 | 36 | 4 |

High-usage sounds label at **15%**, low-usage at **57%**. The relationship is inverse and it makes sense: a sound with a million uses has escaped its origin niche into the general reach-farming population, whose captions are deliberately generic. A sound with 27 uses is still inside the fandom that made it, and those editors tag `#naruto`. **The sounds you most want to characterise are the hardest ones to characterise this way.**

$0.60 per 1,000 attempted is cheap in absolute terms — comparable to the existing pipeline's $1.08-1.45 per 1,000 leads, for a different artifact. The honest figure to plan against is the **effective** one, and at 15% yield in the popular band that is ~$4.00 per 1,000 sounds actually characterised.

## PART 4 — verdict

**Do not close this line, and do not build on it yet.** It is in a genuinely different position from mood (BL-689, blocked on ground truth) and song ID (BL-711, closed on economics):

* **The mechanism is validated.** 12 of 12 concentrated, 0 scattered. Behaviour-derived sound identity is real and observable, and it does not need the track repeats that killed BL-708 — each sound is characterised from its own 12 clips independently, so the 87%-singleton problem never arises. That was the correct insight and it holds.
* **The measurement is thin.** 12 usable sounds, median 3 labelled clips each. This is a promising pilot, not a settled number.
* **The binding constraint is the labeller, not the data source.** The endpoint delivers 12 clips with 68% hashtag coverage for $0.0006. The tag table converts almost none of it in the popular band.

**What would move it:** the 128 clips with caption text but no hashtag, plus the ~200 carrying only reach-farm tags, are not unlabellable — they are unlabelled *by a curated tag table*. Matching franchise and character names against free caption **text** (the `ryangosling`/`spiderman` entries prove the vocabulary already exists), rather than requiring an exact hashtag, is a $0 offline change that could be measured against this same saved corpus before any further spend. If that lifts high-usage coverage from 15% to even 40%, this becomes a real sound-to-content map at $1.50 per 1,000 characterised.

**Recommended next step, in order:** (1) re-run the labeller over the 452 already-saved clips with caption-text matching — $0, no calls; (2) only if coverage clears ~40%, buy another 100 sounds to get per-sound n above 3.
